INSIDE THE WATCH

Watch Technology

A short guide to the movements and features you'll come across in the collection.

Quartz Movement

Quartz watches use a battery to send an electrical current through a tiny quartz crystal, which vibrates at a precise, constant frequency to regulate timekeeping.

This electronic regulation is why quartz watches are typically very accurate and low-maintenance, generally needing only an occasional battery replacement.

Automatic Movement

Automatic watches contain a rotor that spins with the natural motion of the wearer's wrist, winding the mainspring so the watch runs without manual winding or a battery.

Worn regularly, an automatic watch can run continuously; left unworn for a few days, it will need winding or a new burst of wrist motion to restart.

Mechanical Movement

Mechanical watches rely entirely on a system of springs, gears, and an escapement mechanism, hand-wound by turning the crown rather than being powered electronically.

Because every part is mechanical, these movements are often prized for their craftsmanship and are frequently visible through an exhibition case back.

Eco-Drive Technology

Citizen's Eco-Drive technology uses a solar cell beneath the dial to convert light — natural or artificial — into electrical energy that powers the watch.

The energy is stored in a rechargeable cell, so an Eco-Drive watch kept in reasonable light rarely, if ever, needs a battery replacement.

Smart Watch Technology

Smart watches pair with a smartphone to deliver notifications, and use built-in sensors to track activity, heart rate, and sleep, all shown on a digital display.

Unlike traditional movements, smart watches are effectively small computers, so they need regular charging in exchange for their expanded feature set.

Chronograph

A chronograph adds an independent stopwatch function to a regular watch, using extra sub-dials and pushers to start, stop, and reset an elapsed-time measurement.

The main hands keep normal time throughout, while the chronograph hands only move while timing an event, such as a lap or an interval.

Water Resistance

Water-resistance ratings, usually shown in meters, describe the pressure a watch is built to withstand under laboratory conditions, not a literal depth for diving or swimming.

As a general guide, ratings around 30–50m suit splashes only, 100–200m suit swimming, and dedicated dive-rated watches are built and tested for actual diving.
